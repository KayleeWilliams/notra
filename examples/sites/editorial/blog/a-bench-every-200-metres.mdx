My grandmother stopped taking the bus at 78. Not because the bus got worse. The walk to the stop had a hill, and there was nowhere to sit halfway up.

Planners call this the first mile. Riders don't call it anything, because it doesn't feel like part of the trip. But for older people, people with young children and anyone carrying shopping, the first 400 metres decide whether a trip happens at all.

## What the numbers say

In Leeds last year, we counted benches along the walking routes to 60 bus stops in three neighbourhoods. We then compared the counts with boarding data from the operator.

| Benches on the walk to the stop | Stops | Share of boardings by riders over 65 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| None | 22 | 9% |
| One or two | 26 | 14% |
| One every 200 m or closer | 12 | 21% |

The stops with more benches weren't in richer streets or nearer the centre. They were mostly on routes that happened to pass a church, a library or a row of shops with a ledge outside.

> Nobody planned those benches for transit. They were there for other reasons, and they did the work anyway.

## What riders said

Counts only tell you so much, so we also stood at the stops and asked. Of the 112 riders over 65 we spoke to, 41 said they had stopped using at least one bus route in the past five years. The most common reason wasn't the bus. It was the walk to it.

Several described the same routine: they planned trips around places to rest. A pharmacy with a chair by the door. A bus shelter one stop further back, which meant a longer walk but a seat at the end. One man told us he times his trips to the market so he can sit on the church wall while the bells ring.

> People were drawing their own maps of the city, with rest stops on them. Ours didn't have any.

## Why 200 metres

Guidance from several European cities lands on the same figure: many older adults can walk about 200 to 300 metres before they need to rest. A bench every 200 metres turns a 600-metre walk from impossible into three short ones.

## The objection we hear most

Councils worry that benches attract the wrong kind of sitting. Every planner has heard this, and every planner has seen benches removed because of it. The evidence we found doesn't support the worry. In our three neighbourhoods, residents complained about four benches in two years, and all four sat outside late-night takeaways rather than on walking routes.

Design helps too. A bench with armrests at both ends and a back is easier for older people to get up from, which matters more than most people think. A perch, a leaning rail at hip height, takes less space and still lets someone rest for a minute.

## What a city can do this year

Benches are cheap compared with almost anything else in a transport budget. The hard part is knowing where they go.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Map the walks, not the stops">
    Draw the most common walking routes to each stop from the homes around it. The rest points belong on those lines.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Count what's already there">
    Ledges, low walls and church steps count. Mark them before buying anything.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Fill the gaps over 200 metres">
    Start with routes that climb, and with stops that serve clinics or markets.
  </Step>
</Steps>
